              How Stargazer works

              A real sky, from where you stand

              Every star is a real star, in its real place for the place, date and time at the top. What you can see depends on everything that decides it outdoors:

              • Darkness. Daylight and twilight hide all but the brightest stars. Full dark starts when the Sun is 18° below the horizon; the timeline at the top right shows when.
              • Light pollution. Town and city lights brighten the whole sky and wash out faint stars. Far from towns, thousands more appear.
              • The Moon. A bright Moon lights the sky like a small town. The timeline's band shows when it is up.
              • The air. Haze, humidity and clouds dim or hide the stars. Near the horizon, light crosses far more air, so stars there look fainter.
              • The land. Mountains and trees block the low sky. Walk to higher ground or a clearing to see past them.

              The banner under the heading says what is hiding the stars right now, and what you could do about it.

              Reading the numbers

              Magnitude: how bright a star looks

              Astronomers measure brightness in magnitudes, and the scale runs backwards: bigger numbers are fainter. Each step of 1 is about 2.5 times fainter, so 5 steps is 100 times.

              NELM and LM: the faintest star you can see

              The limiting magnitude is the faintest star the sky and your eyes or instrument show straight overhead. NELM (naked-eye limiting magnitude) is for the eye alone; LM is with binoculars or a telescope. "LM 12.1" means stars down to magnitude 12.1 show. Each slot on the hotbar shows its own.

              mag/arcsec²: how dark the sky is

              Sky brightness is measured in magnitudes per square arcsecond, the glow of a tiny patch of sky. Here too bigger is darker. Amateurs measure it with a Sky Quality Meter.

              Where things are in the sky

              The heading strip at the top gives the direction you face as a compass bearing (0° north, 90° east, 180° south, 270° west) and how high you look, in degrees above the horizon (90° is straight up).

              Seeing more

              • Naked eye shows the whole sky, the constellations and the Milky Way.
              • Handheld binoculars (10×50) gather far more light than the eye; they are best for star clusters, the Milky Way and the Moon. They sway a little in your hands.
              • Tripod binoculars (25×100) reach fainter and hold steady. Galaxies and nebulae begin to show.
              • Telescopes show the planets' discs, Saturn's rings, nebulae and galaxies, in a small field. Change the eyepiece with + and − (or the wheel): more power makes things bigger but the field smaller and the sky darker.

              Tripods and telescopes are set up where you stand, on dry, flat, open ground. Aim them by dragging or with the arrow keys. A telescope's field is tiny, so find the target first with its red-dot finder (R), which shows where the tube points, then look through the eyepiece.

              Faint glows look grey, not colored: at night the eye sees little color. Wind and unsteady air blur fine detail at high power.

              Star catalogs

              A star catalog is a list of stars with their positions and brightness, measured by observatories and space missions. The sky is drawn from one:

              • Naked-eye stars: about 26,000 stars to magnitude 7.5, from the HYG Database. Built in; plenty for the eye.
              • Full star set: 2.5 million more, to about magnitude 11.5, from Tycho-2 (ESA's Hipparcos mission). A 20 MB download, for binoculars and telescopes.
              • Deep star set: about 17 million more, from ESA's Gaia mission, for the large telescope. Not in every copy of the pen.

              The catalog in use is named in the sky line at the top left; it says when your instrument could see fainter than the catalog goes. Click or tap it, or open the Menu, to download a catalog or switch between them at any time.

              Ways to play

              Explore is the sky as it is, with guides that name the constellations, the planets and any star you tap.

              The field game takes the guides away. Pick a target from the logbook and find it in the real sky: the card tells you what stands in the way (the horizon, daylight, the land, trees, clouds, or faintness) and when it will be up. Center it and hold steady to log it for points; logged through an eyepiece with its picture in view, it counts in detail for more.

              Draw (C) joins stars with lines to make your own figures. Trace a real constellation from memory and it is named for you, and scores in the game.

              Assists in the menu make things easier (no trees, a flat horizon, clear skies, steady optics); in the game each takes a quarter off the points. Points, awards and figures are kept in this browser.
